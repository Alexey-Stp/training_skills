# Best-practice endurance coaching for triathlon and run-only plans

Research for ticket #5 (part of map #1). Researched 2026-11-19.

**Question.** What does established, well-sourced coaching practice say about building plans for triathlon and for run-only training, and which rules are firm enough to encode versus needing athlete-specific judgement?

**How to read this.** Each topic is split three ways:

- **(a) Encode** — firm enough to write into a skill as a number or hard rule.
- **(b) Judgement** — a defensible range; the skill should pick within it using the athlete profile and recent activities.
- **(c) Contested / weak** — popular claims the skills should not present as fact.

Source tags: **[V]** = abstract or page checked this session; **[M]** = well-known source cited from memory, not re-opened this session (see "Not verified" at the end). References are numbered and listed at the bottom.

Intensity zones in this document use the **three-zone physiological model**: Z1 below the first lactate/ventilatory threshold (LT1/VT1), Z2 between thresholds, Z3 above the second threshold (LT2/VT2, roughly critical power/FTP). This is not the 5- or 7-zone scheme the activity source shows; a mapping is needed (roughly: Z1 = Coggan zones 1-2, Z2 = zones 3-4 up to threshold, Z3 = above threshold).

---

## Headline answer

1. **The evidence supports principles far more firmly than it supports numbers.** The strongest findings are: most training easy; a small number of hard sessions; progress load gradually; cut volume but not intensity before a race; do not make up missed training. Specific percentages (10% rule, 3:1 weeks, 80/20 exactly, ACWR 0.8-1.3) are conventions with weak or mixed direct evidence.
2. **Only tapering has meta-analytic numbers good enough to encode directly** (volume down 41-60%, intensity and frequency held, up to about 2-3 weeks) [1][2][3].
3. **Intensity distribution is firm in direction, not in exact split.** Roughly 80% of sessions easy is a robust description of successful endurance athletes [4]; whether the remainder should be polarized or pyramidal is contested and probably matters little for an amateur [7][8][9].
4. **Periodisation models are tradition more than evidence** [12]. Encode a simple, conventional phase structure as a default and make it responsive to what actually happened.
5. **Load metrics (TSS/CTL/ATL) are useful descriptive bookkeeping, not validated predictors** [22][23]. Use them as guardrails and trend indicators, never as the sole basis for a decision, and track run load separately in triathlon.
6. **The best current injury evidence for running points at single-session spikes**, not week-to-week change or ACWR [18]. This yields one of the few encodable progression rules.
7. **Missed/poor sessions and illness are governed by consensus and coaching convention**, not trials. The conventions are consistent enough across texts to encode as defaults, clearly labelled as such.

## 1. Periodisation over months

**What the sources say.** Classical (Matveyev/Bompa) periodisation moves from general, higher-volume work to race-specific work, then a taper. Friel's triathlon version is Prep → Base 1-3 → Build 1-2 → Peak → Race → Transition, in 3-4 week blocks [30]. Daniels uses four phases of about six weeks for runners [31]. Kiely's review argues that these models rest on shared assumptions that are not well supported, that individual responses vary widely, and that planning should be responsive rather than rule-bound, while not rejecting periodisation outright [12] [V]. A meta-analysis found block periodisation (concentrating one quality for 1-4 weeks) gave small advantages in VO2max (SMD 0.40) and Wmax (SMD 0.28) over traditional organisation, but from six small, short, low-quality studies (mean PEDro 3.7/10) [13] [V]. Mujika et al. frame periodisation as integrating training with recovery, nutrition, skill and psychology rather than as a load template [14] [M].

**(a) Encode**
- A plan toward a goal race is organised backwards from race day: taper → race-specific build → general base. Specificity to race demands (duration, intensity, terrain, bike-run sequencing) increases as the race approaches. This is the one periodisation principle common to every model [12][14][30][31].
- A month plan names its phase and the purpose of each week (load / recovery / taper / race). The exact phase lengths are defaults, not rules.
- A transition/off-season period of reduced, unstructured training follows the season's main race. Duration is judgement (b).

**(b) Judgement**
- Phase lengths. Conventional defaults: base 8-12+ weeks, build 6-8 weeks, peak/taper 1-3 weeks [30][31]. Shorter for an athlete with a deep training history or a short race; longer for a novice or a long-course race.
- Mesocycle length (3 or 4 weeks) and whether the base phase includes some intensity (most modern practice keeps a little intensity year-round [4]).
- Number of A-priority races per year. Texts suggest 2-3 at most, because each requires a taper and recovery [30] [M].

**(c) Contested / weak**
- That any one model (linear, reverse, block, undulating) is superior. Block has a small, low-certainty edge in short studies of trained athletes [13]; nothing shows a meaningful difference for amateurs.
- That a long intensity-free base is required. This is tradition (Lydiard-derived), not a tested finding.

## 2. Weekly structure

**What the sources say.** Autonomic recovery is rapid after sessions below LT1 and delayed after sessions above it; a review puts cardiac-parasympathetic recovery at up to about 24 h after low-intensity work, 24-48 h after threshold work and at least 48 h after high-intensity work [16][17] [M]. Seiler's description of elite practice is about two (sometimes three) high-intensity sessions per week, surrounded by easy volume [4] [V/M]. Coaching texts universally use a hard/easy alternation and one longer endurance session per sport per week [30][31][32].

**(a) Encode**
- Hard sessions (any session whose main set is in Z2-upper or Z3) are not planned on consecutive days by default; leave at least one easy or rest day (about 48 h) between them [17].
- Default cap of **2 hard sessions per week in run-only mode** and **2-3 per week across all three sports combined in triathlon mode** [4]. The cap is across sports, not per sport.
- At least one full rest day (or very easy day) per week as a default for an amateur.
- One long endurance session per week for run and for bike; the long run and long ride are not both followed by a hard day.

**(b) Judgement**
- Sessions per week per sport (see section 6), which days carry the long sessions (driven by the athlete's availability), and whether a third hard session is tolerable.
- Whether a hard swim counts fully against the hard-session cap. Swimming is non-weight-bearing and many coaches treat hard swims as less costly; there is no good evidence either way.
- Double days and their ordering.

**(c) Contested / weak**
- Any specific "ideal week" template. Templates in texts are illustrations, not tested protocols.

## 3. Intensity distribution

**What the sources say.** Seiler's synthesis: nationally and internationally competitive endurance athletes converge on about 80% of sessions at low intensity (around or below 2 mM lactate) and about 20% dominated by high-intensity work; studies intensifying already well-trained athletes show no convincing long-term benefit [4] [V]. Note the unit: this is by **session count** (session-goal method). By **time in zone** the same training is closer to 90% or more in Z1, because hard sessions contain warm-up, recoveries and cool-down [5] [M].

Experimental support: in a 9-week RCT of 48 well-trained athletes, polarized training improved VO2peak (+11.7%) and time to exhaustion more than threshold-, HIIT- or volume-only programmes [6] [V]. In 30 recreational runners over 10 weeks, polarized improved 10 km time by 5.0% versus 3.6% for between-thresholds training; the difference was not significant, only a post-hoc best-compliers subset favoured polarized [10] [V]. In 12 sub-elite runners over 5 months, about 80% versus about 67% of training time in Z1 (Z3 equal at about 8%) favoured the easier distribution in a 10.4 km race [11] [V]. In 12 trained cyclists (crossover, 6 weeks each), a polarized 80/0/20 split improved peak power, lactate threshold and high-intensity capacity more than a 57/43/0 threshold split [58] [V].

Meta-analyses disagree at the margin: Rosenblat et al. (3 RCTs) found polarized beat threshold training for time-trial performance (ES −0.66) [8] [V via secondary]; Silva Oliveira et al. (2024, searched to Oct 2022) found polarized superior only for VO2peak (SMD 0.24), not for time trial, time to exhaustion or threshold power/speed, and the VO2peak edge appeared mainly in interventions under 12 weeks and in highly trained athletes [7] [V]. A formal debate in MSSE ended with Burnley et al. arguing there is no evidence a specifically polarized split is optimal and that pyramidal (lots of Z1, some Z2, least Z3) is at least as defensible [9] [V]. Descriptive reviews find pyramidal is what most elite athletes actually do [15] [M].

Triathlon-specific: in 18 recreational half-Ironman triathletes, both a polarized (about 84/4/11) and a pyramidal (about 78/19/3) distribution improved performance in all three segments; the authors advise not discarding Z2 for recreational half-distance athletes [26] [V]. In nine recreational Ironman athletes followed for 18 weeks, 68% of training time was in Z1; more Z1 time was strongly associated with faster race times (r = −0.92) and a higher share of Z2 time with slower ones (r = 0.94), while the race itself was performed mostly in Z2 (59%) [27] [V] — a very small observational study. That the race is performed in Z2 is the specificity argument for some Z2 work in the build; the correlation is the argument against much of it.

**(a) Encode**
- Target **about 80% of planned sessions as easy (Z1) sessions**, measured by session count; equivalently **at least about 80-85% of weekly training time in Z1**, measured by time in zone. State which unit is being used every time the number appears [4][5].
- Easy means below LT1/VT1 — conversational. An "easy" session review that shows substantial time above LT1 is flagged as intensity drift; this is the most common amateur error the 80/20 literature is aimed at [4].
- The remaining roughly 20% is Z2 and Z3 work. Do not encode a required polarized-versus-pyramidal split.

**(b) Judgement**
- Polarized versus pyramidal emphasis. Reasonable default: pyramidal (more threshold/tempo and race-pace work) for long-course triathlon, marathon and base periods; more Z3 (VO2max-type intervals) for short-course and 5-10 km goals and in the final build [7][9][26].
- How much Z1 share to relax for a time-crunched athlete. The 80/20 pattern is described in athletes training 10-13 sessions per week [4]; an athlete on 4-6 hours per week may reasonably run a lower Z1 share. No good trial settles the number.
- How zones are anchored (lactate, ventilatory, %FTP, %threshold pace, HR). Boundaries from different anchors do not coincide; this needs a decision in the athlete profile.

**(c) Contested / weak**
- "Polarized is optimal" and "avoid Z2 / the grey zone." Not supported by the larger meta-analysis [7], directly disputed [9], and contradicted by recreational triathlete data [26].
- The exact figure 80/20. It is a rounded description of elite practice, not a tested optimum [4].

## 4. Load progression and recovery weeks

**What the sources say.**

*Week-to-week progression.* The 10% rule has been tested: an RCT of 532 novice runners found a graded 13-week programme built on the 10% rule produced the same injury incidence as a standard 8-week programme (20.8% vs 20.3%) [19] [V]. A 1-year cohort of 874 novice runners found those who increased weekly distance by more than 30% over two weeks appeared more vulnerable to distance-related injuries than those under 10% (HR about 1.6, CI crossing 1) [20] [V]. A systematic review found only limited evidence linking changes in training load to running injuries [21] [M].

*Single-session spikes.* An 18-month cohort of 5,205 runners (588,071 sessions, Garmin data, 1,820 injuries) found overuse-injury rate rose when a single run exceeded the **longest run of the previous 30 days by more than 10%**: hazard ratios 1.64 (10-30% over), 1.52 (30-100%), 2.28 (over 100%). In the same data the acute:chronic workload ratio showed an inverse relationship and the week-to-week ratio showed no relationship with injury [18] [V]. Observational and self-reported, but large and device-measured.

*ACWR.* Gabbett's "sweet spot" of 0.8-1.3 and danger above 1.5 [24] [M] came from team sports and was adopted in the IOC consensus on load and injury [25] [V abstract only]. It has since been criticised as conceptually and statistically flawed (ratio artefacts, mathematical coupling, no causal basis) [23] [V], and it did not hold in the runner cohort above [18].

*Recovery weeks.* The IOC/ECSS/ACSM consensus positions are qualitative: overload must be balanced with recovery; inadequate recovery leads to non-functional overreaching [28][29] [V]. The familiar 3:1 (three loading weeks, one reduced) and 2:1 patterns come from coaching texts [30][32]; there is no controlled endurance study establishing an optimal ratio or size of reduction. In trained triathletes, an overload block that pushed athletes into functional overreaching produced *smaller* taper gains and more infections than the same block in athletes who were merely acutely fatigued [33] [V] — evidence against deliberately digging a deep hole.

*Monotony.* Foster linked illness to weeks of high load with little day-to-day variation (high "monotony" and "strain") [34] [M].

**(a) Encode**
- **Single-run spike guardrail:** no planned run longer than **110% of the athlete's longest run in the previous 30 days** [18]. This is the best-supported numeric progression rule available. (Evidence is run-only; applying it to long rides is extrapolation, see (b).)
- **Weekly run volume:** default increase of no more than about 10% over the recent average; never plan an increase above 30% [19][20]. Label the 10% as a conservative convention, the 30% as the evidence-informed ceiling.
- **Never raise volume and intensity sharply in the same week.** Convention across texts [30][31]; consistent with IOC guidance on avoiding rapid load change [25].
- **A recovery week at least every fourth week:** default 3:1, with the recovery week at about 60-70% of the preceding week's volume and the hard-session count reduced (keep some intensity). Label as coaching convention [30][32].
- **No make-up of load after a recovery or missed week** (see section 9).

**(b) Judgement**
- 3:1 versus 2:1. Texts recommend 2:1 for masters athletes, novices, slow recoverers, or high life stress [30] [M].
- Size of the recovery-week reduction (commonly 20-50%).
- Progression rate for bike and swim. Lower impact means lower overuse-injury risk; larger weekly steps are generally tolerated, but there are no good numbers.
- Daniels' alternative for runners: hold a volume for 3-4 weeks, then step up by about one mile (1.6 km) per weekly run, rather than increasing every week [31] [M].
- Long run as a share of weekly volume: Daniels caps at 25-30% of weekly distance or about 150 minutes, whichever is less [31] [M].

**(c) Contested / weak**
- The 10% rule as an injury-prevention rule: tested and not supported in novices [19].
- ACWR thresholds (0.8-1.3 "safe", above 1.5 "danger") as predictive rules for an individual [18][23].
- That planned overreaching improves the subsequent peak [33].

## 5. Load metrics (TSS / CTL / ATL / TSB) and their limits

**What they are.** TSS scales duration by intensity-squared relative to threshold (one hour at threshold = 100). CTL ("fitness") is an exponentially weighted average of daily TSS with a 42-day time constant; ATL ("fatigue") the same with 7 days; TSB ("form") = CTL − ATL [35] [M]. They are a simplified, unfitted version of Banister's impulse-response model [36] [M]. Session-RPE × duration is the validated low-tech equivalent [37] [M].

**Limits documented in the literature.**
- Training-load metrics have not been validated as a measure of training *dose*; most are confounded by duration, and equal scores from different sessions do not imply equal physiological effect [22] [V]. (A published comment argues this critique applies to the metrics, not the concept.)
- Impulse-response models fitted to individuals have wide parameter uncertainty and poor predictive accuracy [38] [M]. The 42/7-day constants are population defaults, not properties of the athlete.
- Everything depends on current thresholds (FTP, threshold pace, CSS, LTHR). A stale threshold silently biases every number.
- TSS-type scores from different sports are not equivalent in tissue stress. 60 TSS of running carries impact loading that 60 TSS of cycling or swimming does not. A single combined CTL can look safe while run load is spiking.
- They ignore non-training stress, sleep, illness and heat, and say nothing about what kind of fitness was built.
- HRV-guided adjustment is not clearly better either: meta-analyses show small, non-significant performance effects versus predefined training, with somewhat fewer non-responders [39][40] [V].
- Consensus statements recommend combining external load, internal load and subjective wellness rather than relying on one metric [25][41].

**(a) Encode**
- Compute and show CTL/ATL/TSB, **and track run load separately from combined load in triathlon mode.**
- Treat the numbers as **descriptive guardrails**: they can trigger a caution or a question, but a plan update is justified by the combination of load trend, session reviews and the athlete's reported state — never by a metric alone [22][41].
- Any advice quoting a load number also states the threshold values it depends on and flags thresholds older than a set age (a profile decision).
- Subjective input (RPE per activity, simple wellness notes) is collected and weighed alongside device data [37][41].

**(b) Judgement**
- CTL ramp rate. Vendor/coach guidance (not peer-reviewed): about 4-8 CTL points per week is described as sustainable for a few weeks, lower for athletes with low CTL; more than that only briefly [42] [V via search summary].
- Race-day TSB. Coaching sources vary, roughly +5 to +25; individual and distance-dependent [42].
- How negative TSB may go in a loading week.

**(c) Contested / weak**
- CTL as "fitness" and TSB as "form" in any predictive sense [22][38].
- Any universal TSB or ramp-rate threshold for injury or overtraining.
- ACWR computed from TSS inherits all ACWR problems [23].

## 6. Balancing swim, bike and run

**What the sources say.** Cross-transfer of aerobic adaptation is partial: it is stronger between cycling and running than to or from swimming, which is highly technique-specific [43][44] [M]. Triathletes carry a large number of sessions, which raises injury, illness and fatigue risk; monitoring should combine internal and external load, and strength work is recommended to offset overuse risk [45] [V abstract]. Most triathlon overuse injuries are attributed to running [46] [M]. British Triathlon's coaching guidelines require individual programmes to reflect the athlete's goals, ability, age, lifestyle, training history and resources [47] [V via search summary].

**(a) Encode**
- Every sport appears in every normal training week in triathlon mode. Minimum practical frequency for maintaining a sport is 2 sessions per week; swim benefits from frequency because it is skill-limited [43][44].
- Run load is progressed and capped by the run-specific rules in section 4, independent of total load.
- Intensity distribution and the hard-session cap (sections 2-3) are applied across the combined week, not per sport.

**(b) Judgement**
- Time split. A common starting point reflects race-time share and injury cost: bike largest (roughly 45-55% of hours), run about 20-30%, swim about 15-25%. Adjust toward the athlete's limiter and away from injury history. No trial establishes an optimal split.
- Which sport receives the hard sessions in a given phase (commonly rotate emphasis; bike tolerates intensity best, run least).
- Using bike volume to build aerobic fitness for an athlete who cannot tolerate more running [43].

**(c) Contested / weak**
- Single-sport "focus blocks" in triathlon — widely used, essentially untested.
- Any claim of full aerobic transfer between sports.

**Strength training** is supported for running and cycling economy and performance [48] [M] and recommended in the triathlon readiness review [45]. Whether and how it appears in plans is a separate open question in the map; it is flagged here only because it competes for the same recovery budget.

## 7. Brick sessions

**What the sources say.** Running after cycling costs more energy than running fresh — a review reports the increase at 1.6% to 11.6%, larger in less able triathletes — alongside altered mechanics and perceived difficulty in the first minutes [49] [V abstract]. The cycle-run transition is one of the most researched parts of the sport and matters most in short-course racing [50] [V abstract]. Direct training evidence is thin: a small study of repeated short bike-run blocks improved the transition itself but not overall performance compared with ordinary training [51] [M]. British Triathlon distributes guidance calling bricks the best race simulation for running off the bike, with bricks becoming shorter and more intense toward the event [52] [V via search summary; the document is vendor-authored].

**(a) Encode**
- Bricks (bike immediately followed by run) are included in triathlon mode during the race-specific build, by default about once per week, and absent from run-only mode.
- The run leg of a brick counts toward run load and the single-run spike rule. A brick with a hard bike or hard run counts as one of the week's hard sessions.
- The off-bike run is kept short in most bricks; the adaptation of interest occurs in the first minutes [49].

**(b) Judgement**
- Brick run duration and intensity (commonly 10-30 minutes; longer race-pace bricks for long-course in the final build), frequency in base phase, and whether to use multiple short bike-run repeats for short-course [51].
- Less experienced triathletes show a larger transition cost [49] and plausibly benefit from more brick practice.

**(c) Contested / weak**
- That bricks improve overall race performance beyond ordinary training. Supported by race specificity and coaching consensus, not by trials [51].
- Long run-off-the-bike sessions as necessary Ironman preparation — convention; they are costly to recover from.

## 8. Tapering

**What the sources say.** Bosquet et al. (27 studies): the best performance gains come from about a **2-week** taper with volume **exponentially reduced by 41-60%**, with **no reduction in intensity or frequency** [1] [V]. Wang et al. (2023, 14 endurance-only studies): tapers of **up to 21 days** with volume reduced 41-60%, intensity and frequency maintained, improved time-trial performance (SMD −0.45); both progressive and step tapers worked; tapers preceded by an overload period showed larger gains [2] [V]. Mujika's triathlon guidance: volume down 41-60%, intensity maintained, frequency kept above 80% of normal, individual optimal duration anywhere from 4 to 28 days, with travel, heat and altitude built into the plan [3] [V abstract]. Tapers do not change VO2max or economy; the gain is fatigue removal [2]. Among recreational marathoners, disciplined tapers of up to 3 weeks were associated with better finish times than short or erratic ones [53] [M]. The overload finding must be read with [33]: overload helps only if it stops short of overreaching.

**(a) Encode**
- For an A-priority race: reduce weekly volume progressively to **40-60% below** pre-taper volume (i.e. the final week at roughly 40-60% of normal) [1][2][3].
- **Keep intensity**: retain short race-pace and above-race-pace efforts; shorten the sessions, do not soften them [1][2][3].
- **Keep frequency at 80% or more** of normal sessions; in triathlon keep all three sports in the taper [3].
- Taper length between **1 and 3 weeks**; never longer than 3 [1][2].
- Progressive (gradually decreasing) reduction is the default pattern [1][2].
- No new training stimulus, no fitness tests and no load make-up inside the taper.

**(b) Judgement**
- Length by race and athlete: conventionally about 1 week for sprint/Olympic and 10 km-half marathon, up to 2 for half-distance triathlon, 2-3 for Ironman and marathon [30][32]; longer after heavier preceding load and for the run than for the swim [3] [M for the per-sport point].
- B- and C-priority races: a few easier days rather than a taper.
- Race-day TSB target (section 5).
- Post-race recovery duration before structured training resumes — convention only (rough guide: days for a sprint, 2-4 weeks for Ironman/marathon).

**(c) Contested / weak**
- A deliberate overreaching block before the taper [2] versus [33].
- A late 20-30% load re-increase in the final days: suggested by modelling work cited in [3], not established.

## 9. Adjusting after missed sessions, poor sessions and illness

**What the sources say.** There are no trials of plan-repair strategies; this area rests on detraining physiology, consensus statements and coaching texts.

*Detraining.* Short breaks cost little: measurable declines in VO2max and blood volume appear in highly trained athletes within the first weeks of stopped or insufficient training and are milder in the recently trained; reduced training retains adaptations far better than cessation [54] [V abstract]. Muscle oxidative enzyme activity falls with a half-time of about 12 days of complete cessation, while VO2max remains above untrained levels even after 84 days [55] [V via search summary].

*Missed sessions* (Friel's widely reproduced rules [56] [V]): up to 3 days missed — resume as if nothing happened and do not make sessions up; 4-7 days — treat as an unplanned recovery week, re-plan, and rescue only the two or three most important sessions; 1-2 weeks — step back a block, repeat the last hard week before moving on, accept dropping 1-3 weeks of planned training; more than 2 weeks — return to base and remove part of the build.

*Poor sessions and overreaching.* The ECSS/ACSM consensus defines functional overreaching (short-term decrement, recovers in days to about two weeks), non-functional overreaching (weeks to months) and overtraining syndrome (months), states that no single marker is diagnostic, and requires ruling out illness, energy and carbohydrate deficiency, iron deficiency and similar causes first [28] [V].

*Illness.* The IOC consensus identifies poor load management as a risk factor for acute illness [29] [V abstract]. A systematic review for the IOC found acute respiratory illness symptoms last a mean 7.1 days, about 20% of episodes cost more than one day of training, reported return times were 0-8.5 days, and data are limited and highly variable between individuals [57] [V]. The "neck check" (train lightly if symptoms are above the neck only) is traditional guidance without direct scientific validation [57 and associated commentary] [V via search summary]. Avoiding exercise with fever or systemic symptoms is standard sports-medicine advice because of the risk of complications including myocarditis.

**(a) Encode**
- **Never make up missed load** by stacking sessions or adding to later days. A missed planned session is dropped, or, if it was a key session, swapped with an easy session later in the same week provided the hard-day spacing rule still holds [56].
- **Gap-length brackets** (convention, from [56]): 1-3 days → continue the plan unchanged; 4-7 days → treat the gap as the recovery week and re-plan the mesocycle; 8-14 days → repeat the last completed loading week at reduced load before progressing; more than 14 days → step back a phase and rebuild; the race date does not move, so planned build weeks are deleted rather than compressed.
- **After any gap of a week or more, the single-run spike rule (section 4) is re-evaluated against the last 30 days**, which automatically lowers the allowed long run.
- **Illness hard stop:** fever, chest symptoms, muscle aches, gastrointestinal illness or any systemic symptom → no training; the skill advises rest and, for anything beyond a mild short illness, medical advice. The skills do not diagnose.
- **Return from illness:** first sessions easy (Z1) and shortened; no hard session until the athlete has trained easily without symptoms recurring and HR/RPE at usual easy pace is back to normal [56][57]. A reasonable default is one easy day per day of illness before the first hard session — label as convention.
- **A single poor session changes nothing.** A pattern does: two or more failed key sessions inside about a week, or elevated HR/RPE at usual outputs across several sessions → convert the next hard session(s) to easy and insert rest. Under-performance persisting more than about two weeks despite reduced load → recommend medical review rather than more plan changes [28].
- Missed sessions in the taper or race week are simply dropped.

**(b) Judgement**
- Whether above-the-neck-only symptoms allow light Z1 training. Common practice and tolerated by many athletes, but unvalidated [57]; the conservative default is rest or very easy only, with the athlete deciding.
- How fast to return to full load (individual; depends on illness severity and pathogen) [57].
- Which missed sessions count as "key", and whether a race goal should be downgraded after a long gap.
- Causes of a poor session: heat, fuelling, sleep, life stress, stale thresholds — to be checked before attributing it to fitness or fatigue [28].

**(c) Contested / weak**
- The neck check as a validated rule [57].
- Any fixed day-count for return after illness.
- HRV or resting-HR thresholds as triggers for skipping a session [39][40].

## 10. Run-only mode: what changes

- Sections 1-5, 8 and 9 apply unchanged. Sections 6 and 7 drop out.
- With no low-impact sports to carry aerobic volume, run-specific load limits bind earlier: the single-run spike rule [18], the weekly progression limits [19][20] and the long-run share cap [31] become the primary constraints on the plan.
- Hard-session cap: 2 per week by default (section 2). Daniels additionally caps quality volume per week as a share of weekly distance — about 10% at threshold pace, 8% at interval (VO2max) pace, 5% at repetition pace [31] [M]. These are coaching rules, usable as (b) defaults.
- Marathon taper conventionally 2-3 weeks [32][53], inside the encoded 1-3 week range.
- Optional cross-training (bike, swim) is a judgement tool for adding aerobic load without impact [44].

## 11. Candidate default rule set

A compact list for the later decision ticket. "Basis" indicates how hard the rule is.

| # | Rule | Default value | Basis |
|---|------|---------------|-------|
| 1 | Easy share of sessions | about 80% of sessions in Z1 (or 80-85%+ of time) | Descriptive + RCTs, direction firm [4][6][7] |
| 2 | Hard sessions per week | max 2 (run-only), max 2-3 total (triathlon) | Elite practice + recovery physiology [4][17] |
| 3 | Spacing of hard sessions | not on consecutive days (about 48 h) | Recovery physiology [16][17] |
| 4 | Single-run spike | no run over 110% of longest run in last 30 days | Large cohort [18] |
| 5 | Weekly run volume increase | default up to 10%, never over 30% | Convention / cohort [19][20] |
| 6 | Recovery week | every 4th week (3:1), about 60-70% volume | Coaching convention [30][32] |
| 7 | Long run share | at most 25-30% of weekly run distance | Coaching text [31] |
| 8 | Taper volume | final-week volume 40-60% below normal, progressive | Meta-analyses [1][2] |
| 9 | Taper intensity and frequency | intensity kept; frequency at least 80% | Meta-analyses [1][2][3] |
| 10 | Taper length | 1-3 weeks by race distance | Meta-analyses + convention [1][2] |
| 11 | Missed load | never made up; gap brackets 1-3 / 4-7 / 8-14 / 15+ days | Coaching convention [56] |
| 12 | Illness | systemic symptoms → no training; easy-only return | Clinical convention [29][57] |
| 13 | Load metrics | shown and used as guardrails only; run load tracked separately | Validity critiques [22][23] |
| 14 | Triathlon balance | all three sports weekly, at least 2 sessions each | Cross-transfer limits [43][44] |
| 15 | Bricks | about weekly in race-specific build; run leg counts as run load | Specificity [49][51] |

Rules 1-5 and 8-10 are the best-supported. Rules 6, 7, 11, 12, 14 and 15 are conventions the skills should label as defaults that the athlete can override.

## Not verified / limits of this research

- Items tagged **[M]** were cited from memory of well-known sources and not re-opened this session: [5], [14], [15], [16], [17], [21], [24] (thresholds; abstract was checked), [30]-[32], [34]-[38], [43], [44], [46], [48], [51], [53]. Their existence is not in doubt, but specific figures attributed to them (for example Daniels' percentage caps, the 24/48 h recovery windows, the ACWR 0.8-1.3 band) should be checked against the originals before being encoded.
- Coaching books [30][31][32][35] are not available online; page-level claims were not checked.
- PubMed and PMC blocked direct page fetches; most **[V]** items were verified through search-returned abstracts or Europe PMC abstract text, not full text. Full texts of the IOC consensus statements [25][29] were not read, so their specific numeric recommendations are not quoted.
- Governing-body material was thin. British Triathlon's public documents are mostly coach-governance rules; the 10%-rule mention in an older (2022) coaching guideline and the brick guidance were seen only in search summaries. No USA Triathlon or World Triathlon coaching curriculum was accessible (they sit behind coach-education paywalls).
- The CTL ramp-rate and race-day TSB figures come from vendor and coach articles, not peer-reviewed work.
- How intervals.icu computes load per sport and what form bands it displays were not established here; that belongs to the activity-source tickets.
- Almost all intensity-distribution and taper trials use trained, mostly male, mostly young athletes over 4-12 weeks. Applicability to one amateur over a season is an extrapolation in every case.
